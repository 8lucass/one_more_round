# This Is the Teenage Brain on Video Games

Lucas A.
Block B

I made a short browser game, *One More Round*, about the moment when a game asks if you want to play again. You steer a ship through a tunnel for 30 seconds, and choosing a rematch moves a fictional bedroom clock from 10:30 PM to 11:00 PM. Each part of the game connects to one brain structure, although scores and the clock are only game data: they can illustrate the research, but they cannot measure anyone's brain.

The striatum is a group of structures deep in the brain that uses dopamine, a chemical messenger that may be involved in learning and reinforcement. Koepp et al. (1998) found evidence consistent with dopamine release in the striatum while eight adult men played a video game, so their study does not show what happens in teenagers. In a study that included teenagers, Galvan et al. (2006) found that the nucleus accumbens, a reward area in the striatum, was more active relative to the prefrontal cortex than in children or adults. In my game, sounds, a "BANKED +500" flash and "Beat [your score]?" give instant feedback that can make one more round tempting.

The prefrontal cortex is responsible for planning, prioritizing and making good decisions, and it is one of the last parts of the brain to mature (National Institute of Mental Health [NIMH], 2023). Every +500 in my game needs this kind of planning: take 200 safe points, or grab the bonus and escape the barrier that arrives less than a second later.

The suprachiasmatic nucleus, in the hypothalamus, is the brain's master clock (Patton & Hastings, 2018), and it controls when melatonin, the sleep hormone, is made (National Institute of General Medical Sciences, 2023). In teens, melatonin stays high later at night (NIMH, 2023). The fictional clock jumping to 11:00 PM shows how each extra round can push bedtime later.
